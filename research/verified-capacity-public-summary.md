## Can greater financial capacity make cooperation less likely?

Many useful projects require several organizations to finance a common input. Examples include a shared environmental audit, a certification dossier, a monitoring system, a technical study, or a dataset that can be reused by multiple participants.

A natural approach is to assign contributions partly according to each participant’s ability to pay. A participant with greater documented financial capacity can contribute more and may make the project possible.

But this creates a potential tension. The same information that shows that a participant can pay more may also cause the cost-sharing rule to assign that participant a larger contribution. A pivotal participant may therefore become essential to financing the project while simultaneously becoming less willing to join it.

This paper studies that tension. I call it the **verified-capacity paradox**: documented payment capacity can first facilitate collective provision and then discourage the participation needed to sustain it.

## The shared-cost problem

Consider a group that wants to purchase a common evidence package from an external provider. The package has a fixed procurement cost. Once produced, it can be used by all members of the consortium.

The group contains two types of participants:

- an **anchor**, whose relatively large documented payment capacity is important for financing the package; and
- several **fringe participants**, each of whom has more limited payment capacity.

The term “fringe” does not mean that these participants are unimportant. It indicates that each is individually smaller than the anchor and contributes a more limited amount to the common cost.

A coalition is financially viable when the members’ combined documented capacities are sufficient to cover the procurement cost.

If the anchor has documented capacity $\ell$, each fringe participant has capacity $\ell_F$, and a coalition contains $q$ fringe participants, financial viability requires

$$
\ell+q\ell_F\geq C,
$$

where $C$ is the total procurement cost.

Greater anchor capacity therefore makes it easier to finance the package.

## Why capacity can also discourage participation

The consortium uses a cost-sharing constitution to allocate the procurement cost among its members. Contributions cannot exceed each participant’s documented payment capacity.

When the anchor’s payment constraint binds, its assigned contribution equals its documented capacity:

$$
t_A=\ell.
$$

The anchor’s net payoff from membership is then

$$
b_A-\ell,
$$

where $b_A$ is the anchor’s benefit from the shared package before subtracting its contribution.

Before signing the accession agreement, the anchor can instead remain outside the consortium and receive a pre-accession alternative payoff, denoted by $v_A$. The anchor joins only when membership is at least as attractive as remaining outside:

$$
b_A-\ell\geq v_A.
$$

Thus, while greater documented capacity helps the group cover the procurement cost, it also increases the contribution assigned to the anchor. Beyond a certain point, the anchor prefers not to join.

## An intermediate range of viable cooperation

The model identifies two capacity thresholds.

The lower threshold is

$$
\underline{\ell}=C-n\ell_F,
$$

where $n$ is the total number of potential fringe participants. Below this threshold, even the anchor and all fringe participants together cannot finance the package.

The upper participation threshold is

$$
\ell^P=b_A-v_A.
$$

Above this threshold, the anchor’s assigned contribution is so large that it prefers its pre-accession alternative.

Under the paper’s Intermediate Extractability Assumption, positive coalition provision can therefore be supported only when

$$
\underline{\ell}\leq\ell\leq\ell^P.
$$

This creates three regions:

1. **Low documented capacity:** the package cannot be financed.
2. **Intermediate documented capacity:** a positive coalition can form and procure the package.
3. **High documented capacity:** the anchor is assigned too much of the cost and refuses accession.

Greater financial capacity is therefore not necessarily monotonically beneficial for cooperation.

## A concrete institutional interpretation

Consider several agricultural projects that need a common environmental verification dossier before lenders, buyers, or regulators will recognize their environmental performance.

The consortium hires an accredited auditor or technical provider to collect data, conduct measurements, and prepare the dossier. The dossier is a shared input: once produced, several projects can use it.

A large cooperative, lead exporter, development-finance institution, or major project may serve as the anchor. Smaller producers or projects form the fringe.

Prospective members first sign an accession agreement and commit documented payment capacity. The consortium’s constitution then allocates the cost, after which the provider is formally commissioned.

Documented capacity may be based on an audited balance-sheet measure, a contractual escrow balance, a certified credit limit, an ownership share, or another externally verifiable measure. The model assumes that this measure is contractible and cannot be freely understated after the accession process begins.

The anchor is important because the smaller participants may be unable to finance the evidence package without it. Yet a constitution that assigns costs according to documented capacity may place a disproportionate burden on the anchor and cause it to remain outside.

## Coalition size and outsider access

The smallest group that can cover the procurement cost is the **minimum viable coalition**.

A larger coalition spreads the remaining cost across more fringe members. This makes membership more attractive to existing fringe participants. However, potential outsiders may also benefit from the evidence without joining, especially when the evidence is portable across projects, lenders, or regulatory settings.

The value of remaining outside therefore affects coalition size:

- when outsider access is limited, the grand coalition is easier to support;
- when outsiders can obtain substantial value without contributing, a smaller minimum viable coalition may be supported;
- for intermediate outsider value, more than one positive coalition may be stable.

A no-provision equilibrium may still coexist with a positive coalition because several participants may need to join together. One participant acting alone may be unable to make the package financially viable.

## What the paper contributes

The paper connects three elements that are often studied separately:

1. documented payment capacity;
2. negotiated or constitutionally assigned cost shares; and
3. voluntary coalition membership.

The main contribution is not simply that liquidity constraints affect bargaining. It is that the same capacity-sensitive cost-sharing rule can determine whether a voluntary public-good coalition exists.

The model therefore provides a possible explanation for why a financially strong organization may decline to anchor a consortium even when its participation is necessary for the project to proceed.

## What the model does—and does not—show

This is a theoretical paper. It studies a fixed-cost common input and derives the conditions under which positive coalition provision can be supported. A numerical example illustrates that the intermediate-capacity region can be nonempty.

The model does not imply that greater financial capacity generally reduces cooperation. The result depends on an institution in which:

- payment capacity is documented and contractible;
- the cost-sharing rule responds to that capacity;
- the anchor loses its pre-accession alternative after joining;
- the common package has a substantial fixed cost; and
- the anchor is pivotal to financing it.

The mechanism may not apply when participants can freely conceal capacity, when assigned contributions are independent of capacity, or when greater capacity substantially increases the quality or value of the common input.

## Implications for institutional design

The analysis suggests that consortium constitutions should distinguish between a participant’s ability to pay and the amount it can be asked to contribute without undermining accession.

Possible institutional responses include:

- limiting the share assigned to a pivotal participant;
- using matching contributions rather than assigning the full documented capacity;
- obtaining external co-financing for the fixed cost;
- establishing assurance mechanisms that coordinate simultaneous accession;
- reducing the procurement cost through shared standards or reusable evidence infrastructure.

These approaches are not formally ranked in the baseline model. They illustrate how institutional design can preserve the financing benefits of documented capacity without making the pivotal participant unwilling to join.

## The main message

Documented financial capacity can have two opposing effects on collective action.

It can make a shared project financially feasible. But when the same capacity determines assigned contributions, it can also make participation unattractive to the organization whose involvement is essential.

The challenge is therefore not simply to identify who can pay. It is to design cost-sharing institutions that use financial capacity without extracting so much from pivotal participants that the coalition collapses.
